Estimating a Concrete Pad

Part 0

Why Estimate?

A concrete pad (or slab) is a flat layer of concrete poured on the ground. It's what you see under sheds, garages, patios, driveways, and many houses. Building one takes three main materials:

  • Forms: a wooden frame around the edge that holds the wet concrete in shape until it hardens.
  • Rebar: a grid of steel bars inside the slab that keeps it from cracking apart.
  • Concrete: delivered wet by a mixer truck and poured into the forms, where it hardens into the pad.

Before anyone pours a slab, someone has to figure out exactly what to order and what it will cost. On many projects, concrete alone is 15–20% of the total cost. Getting it wrong is expensive either way. Order too little, and the crew has to stop mid-pour and wait for another truck while the concrete already placed starts to set. Order too much, and you pay for concrete you can't use and then have to get rid of it.

In this tutorial, you'll learn the method by working through an example pad. Then you'll practice on random pads until you've got it. After that, you'll estimate your own utility shed pad.

What you'll be able to do

  • Calculate how much concrete a pad needs and how much to order
  • Lay out and count the rebar for a pad
  • Figure out the lumber for the forms
  • Put it all together into a cost estimate

Part 1

Concrete Volume

Our example pad: 10 ft × 14 ft, 4 in. thick

Step 1: Get everything into the same unit.

Two dimensions are in feet and one is in inches. You can't multiply them together until they match. Convert the thickness to feet:

4 in. ÷ 12 = 4/12 ft = 0.333… ft

Keep the fraction (4/12, or 1/3) if you can. Rounding to 0.33 makes your answer a little low.

Step 2: Find the volume.

Volume = Length × Width × Thickness

Step 3: Convert to cubic yards.

Concrete is sold by the cubic yard (yd3). A cubic yard is a cube 3 ft on each side, so it holds 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 cubic feet.

To convert, divide by 27.

Part 2

How Much to Order

Ready-mix concrete is ordered in ½ cubic yard steps: 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and so on. You can't order 1.73, so you have to pick a step.

Always round up. Coming up short in the middle of a pour is the worst-case scenario.

Step 4: Concrete cost.

Multiply the amount you're ordering by the price per cubic yard.

Example price for this tutorial: $165 per yd3

Did you know? Most ready-mix suppliers charge an extra "short load" fee for small orders. On a small pad, that fee can make the price per yard look very different. You'll come back to this when you estimate your shed.

Part 3

Rebar Layout

Concrete is very strong when you squeeze it (compression) but weak when you pull on it (tension). Steel reinforcing bar, called rebar, is set into the slab to handle the pulling forces.

For our pads, the rebar follows three rules:

  1. 3 in. edge distance. Bars stop 3 inches short of the edge of the concrete, so steel near the surface doesn't rust.
  2. No more than 24 in. apart. Bars form a grid, and no two bars can be more than 2 feet apart.
  3. A bar at each edge. The first and last bar in each direction sit right at the 3 in. edge distance.

Step 5: Find the usable area.

Take 3 inches off each side. That's 6 inches (0.5 ft) off each dimension.

Step 6: Count the bars in each direction.

This is the step people mix up most often, so go slowly.

Bars that run the long way (parallel to the 14 ft side) are spaced out across the short side. Bars that run the short way are spaced out across the long side.

To count the bars in one direction:

  • Spaces = distance you're spacing across ÷ 2 ft, rounded up
  • Bars = spaces + 1 (there's always one more bar than spaces, like fence posts)

Bars running the long way: spaced across 9.5 ft
9.5 ÷ 2 = 4.75 → 5 spaces → 6 bars, each 13.5 ft long

Now you try the other direction.

Part 4

Rebar Pieces and Laps

Rebar comes in 10 ft lengths. Some of the bar runs you just counted are longer than that.

  • If a bar run is 10 ft or less, one 10 ft bar covers it.
  • If a bar run is longer than 10 ft, you need a full 10 ft bar plus a cut piece. The two overlap by 6 inches and are wired together. This overlap is called a lap, and it lets the two pieces act like one bar.

Step 7: How long is the cut piece?

The full bar covers 10 ft. The cut piece covers the rest of the run, plus the 6 in. lap.

Cut length = run − 10 + 0.5

Step 8: Cut from as few bars as possible.

You don't need a whole new bar for every cut piece. Get as many cut pieces as you can out of each 10 ft bar:

  • Pieces per bar = 10 ÷ cut length, rounded down (you can't use part of a piece)
  • Bars for cut pieces = number of cut pieces ÷ pieces per bar, rounded up

For the example pad: 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 → 2 pieces per bar, with 2 ft left over.

Step 9: Count the bars.

Runs the short way (9.5 ft): each fits in one bar → 8 runs × 1 = 8 bars
Runs the long way (13.5 ft): 6 full bars + 3 bars for the cut pieces = 9 bars

Step 10: Rebar cost.

Example price: $9.50 per 10 ft bar of #4 rebar

Part 5

Forms

Before the concrete is poured, a wooden frame called a form is built around the edge of the pad to hold the wet concrete in place until it hardens.

For our pads, the forms are made from 2×6 lumber, one board per side.

Wait, why a 2×6 for a 4 in. slab?

Lumber names are nominal sizes, not the real measurements. A "2×4" is actually 1½ in. × 3½ in., which is too short for a 4 in. slab. A 2×6 is actually 1½ in. × 5½ in., so it gets ripped (cut lengthwise) down to 4 in.

Step 11: Pick the boards.

2×6 boards are sold in 8, 10, 12, 14, and 16 ft lengths. For each side, pick the shortest board that's at least as long as that side. If a side isn't a stock length, buy the next size up and cut it.

The example pad needs:

  • 2 boards for the 10 ft sides → 2 × 10 ft
  • 2 boards for the 14 ft sides → 2 × 14 ft

Step 12: Form cost.

Example prices: 2×6×10 = $8.50, 2×6×14 = $12.50
